Decoding pipeline — pull model

How decoded video frames and audio buffers flow from FFmpeg to the sinks, how the two streams stay synchronized, and where backpressure lives now that channels are bounded(1).

ADR-0030 (Crossbar frame contracts) · ADR-0032 (pull-shape controller) · ADR-0033 (HW decode) · ADR-0035 (IMasterClock split) · ADR-0036 (decode/playback decoupling). Generated 2026-05-12.

It shipped as FrameFlow.Graph.IClockSource — a published signal rather than an inherited pull — per the proposal in decoding-pipeline-proposed.html. The rest of the pull-model description still holds.

The short version

The decode side and the playback side are two separate subsystems joined by a pair of bounded(1) channels exposed as FramePipeline<T>. Decode produces, playback consumes. Nothing in the decode layer knows about clocks, sinks, pause, or AV-sync. Synchronization is applied between the seam and the sink, not inside the decoder.

The pull shape changes how synchronization works compared to the old push pipeline:

  • Audio is presented "as fast as the sink can accept it." OpenAL's sample-counter is the master clock — buffers go in, the sound card consumes them at the real sample rate, and GetPlaybackTime() returns how many seconds of audio have actually been played.
  • Video is gated by a PacedAgainst(clock, strategy) Crossbar operator inserted between stream.Video and the video sink. For each frame it computes delay = framePts − clock.Position and await Task.Delay(delay) before letting the frame through.
  • The "are we ahead or behind?" question is answered by the master clock, which is the audio sink when audio is being played, and a wallclock-backed IReadOnlyPlaybackClock otherwise.
  • Backpressure is the bounded(1) channel. A slow video sink causes the video decode worker to block on WriteAsync, which causes the demux pump to block on its packet queue, which causes the source reader to slow down — all without explicit cooperation.

How A/V sync actually works

The two streams never explicitly "wait for each other." Synchronization emerges from two independent feedback loops, both reading the same master clock.

Audio side no operator

RunAudioPumpAsync is the simplest possible composition:

await stream.Audio.ToSinkAsync(audioSink, ct);

The pump pulls a PcmAudioBuffer, hands it to OpenAlAudioSink.WriteAsync, and OpenAL queues it onto the sound device. The sound card consumes samples at the real PCM rate, advancing the sink's sample counter. When asked, the sink returns samples_played / sample_rate as its GetPlaybackTime(). That is the master clock.

The audio pump runs as fast as WriteAsync lets it, which is governed by OpenAL's internal buffer fullness — that's the natural rate limiter. No Task.Delay on the audio path.

Video side PacedAgainst

RunVideoPumpAsync inserts one operator between the stream and the sink:

await stream.Video
    .PacedAgainst(masterClock, syncStrategy)
    .Observe(metrics)
    .ToSinkAsync(videoSink, ct);

For each pulled IVideoFrame, PacedAgainst calls strategy.GetVideoDelay(frame.Pts, clock.GetPlaybackTime()) and awaits the result before letting the frame through. The strategy's job is "given where the master timeline is, how long should this frame wait?"

If the video is ahead of the clock, the operator delays. If it's behind, delay is zero and the frame goes straight through — the sink presents back-to-back frames until video catches up.

Master clock selection

The video pump asks PipelineController.MasterClockForVideo() for the clock to pace against. Two cases:

Source has audio?Clock returnedWhy
Yes (and audio sink registered) ConservativeAudioMasterClock Wraps the audio sink's GetPlaybackTime() and the wallclock together, returning min(audio, wall). The min guards against the audio clock running ahead during sink activation glitches (audio buffers queued but not yet playing).
No audio stream ReadOnlyClockAdapter Forwards the playback session's wallclock. The audio sink's sample counter would be stuck at zero (no buffers ever arrive) and would stall video forever if used.

Both adapters implement IMasterClock — a single-method interface (GetPlaybackTime()) extracted from IAudioSink by ADR-0035 precisely so the PacedAgainst operator could couple to "a clock" rather than "an audio sink."

Backpressure — why bounded(1) is enough

Each channel's capacity is one. The FullMode is Wait, not DropOldest. This means the producer blocks on a full channel — and that block ripples all the way back to the source reader.

Video sink is slow (presenting a frame takes longer than the decode rate) │ ▼ RunVideoPumpAsync stuck inside videoSink.PresentAsync │ ▼ PacedAgainst already produced; no read happens │ ▼ Channel<IVideoFrame> (1) full — has one frame buffered │ ▼ RunVideoDecodeAsync blocks on channel.Writer.WriteAsync │ ▼ VideoDecoder stops draining decoded frames │ ▼ VideoDecoder.PacketQueue fills up │ ▼ DemuxPump blocks on SendPacketAsync(video, …) │ ▼ IDemuxSession stops calling av_read_frame — source idles

The audio path has the same shape. Critically, audio and video backpressure independently: if the video sink stalls, audio keeps playing through cleanly because they share only the demux pump's packet routing, not the channels or the workers. The demux pump routes whichever side has free queue space.

Pause is free

Because backpressure is end-to-end, pause is just "stop pulling." When PipelineController.PauseWorkersAsync cancels the episode CTS, both pumps exit their ToSinkAsync loops. The channels fill, the decode workers block on WriteAsync, the demux pump blocks on the packet queue — the whole pipeline quiesces with no extra gate primitive. Resume just spawns fresh pump tasks, and they drain whatever's buffered and continue.

Lifecycle behaviors

EventWhat happens at the seam
Play / first start Stream's _epochCts already running (constructed at CreateAsync). Controller spawns video + audio pumps, both immediately start await foreach over the channel-backed pipelines.
Pause Controller cancels episode CTS. Pumps exit. Decode side keeps running until channels fill, then naturally blocks. Decoders, demuxer, and channels all stay alive.
Resume Controller spawns fresh pump tasks on a new episode CTS. Pumps immediately drain whatever buffered frames the channels hold. EOF latch is cleared.
Seek Controller pauses pumps first, then calls stream.SeekAsync(t). The stream cancels its epoch CTS (separate from the controller's episode CTS), waits for decode workers to exit, drains pre-seek frames out of the channels, calls decoder.Flush() + demux.SeekAsync(t), then starts a fresh epoch with the same channel objects. Controller then re-spawns pumps.
EOF Demux pump returns naturally. Stream finalizes decoders (drains any flushed trailing frames), calls channel.Writer.TryComplete(). Pump await foreachs exit. Stream fires EndOfStreamReached; controller waits for pumps to drain then re-fires its own EOF event so the state machine transitions to Ended only after the last frame was presented.
Loop restart (EOF then seek) EOF completed the channels. SeekAsync detects this and constructs new channel objects so the new epoch can write again. Pull consumers iterating the public FramePipeline see the iteration restart cleanly because stream.Video resolves the channel on each iteration start.

Pull consumers can skip playback entirely

The big payoff of ADR-0036's split: anything that wants decoded frames without playback uses IDecodedMediaStream directly and never instantiates a PlaybackController:

await using var stream = await factory.CreateAsync(source, ct);

// Live captioning — pull audio, run Whisper, no AV-sync, no audio sink
await foreach (var pcm in stream.Audio.TranscribeWithWhisper(model))
    Console.WriteLine(pcm.Text);

// YOLO inference — pull video frames at decode rate, no pacing
await foreach (var det in stream.Video.ToCpu().DetectWith(yolo))
    Render(det);

These consumers see no pacing. stream.Video emits as fast as the decoder produces; stream.Audio the same. Backpressure still works — if Whisper takes 800ms per buffer, the audio decoder blocks until Whisper consumes — but there's no master clock and no Task.Delay. The pull consumer's own consumption rate paces the whole pipeline.

When playback is wanted alongside inference, the pipeline can be tee'd: controller.AudioBuffers.TeeTo(audioSink).TranscribeWithWhisper(model). The audio sink keeps the master clock alive for video pacing, and Whisper gets a parallel copy of every buffer.

One-line summary

Decode produces frames into bounded(1) channels; audio plays through to the sound card, whose sample counter is the master clock; video reads that clock once per frame via PacedAgainst and inserts the right Task.Delay before presenting; if any consumer slows down, the bounded channel propagates the slowdown all the way back to the source.
